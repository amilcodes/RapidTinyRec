MiniOneRec · Qwen2-1.5B · RTX A6000 + H100 · updated Nov 9 (fourth session)

RapidTinyRec Speedrun

How fast can an LLM generative recommender decode, and is beam search even the right algorithm? Batch-1 latency on the frozen 500-user Office cohort; quality on all 4,866 Office and 4,533 Industrial test users.

Findings so far

  • Catalog scaling (synthetic, real model): on denser catalogs of 10k to 1M SIDs scored by the released model, the minimal set of prefixes any bound-based decoder must expand stays flat (about 8.4 / 42 / 410 for top-10 / 50 / 500), certified top-10 at M = 16 holds for at least 99.6% of users (100% at M = 32), and latency at a fixed budget does not move (6.34 to 6.42 ms on an A6000 vs 6.38 ms on the real catalog). The two-sided guarantee loosens as prefixes get more children; the certificate does not.
  • End-to-end fusion: SASRec now runs inside the decoder's CUDA graph on a side stream, so the request carries only the user's 20-item history. Office 2.89 ms (2.66 ms with precomputed SASRec rows, same allocation), Industrial 3.09 ms (3.07 ms); quality identical; FP8 throughput unchanged (3,134 / 3,660 users/s at B = 128).
  • Industrial certified top-500 with fusion reaches 100% of users at M = 512 (4.23 ms; 83% at M = 256).
  • Negative: diversity caps (at most 1 to 3 items per category) do not need exact search here; the LLM's top 50 already spans about 25 first codes, so top-50-then-diversify recovers 99.7 to 100% of the exact capped top-10. A torch.compile'd SASRec head returned wrong values inside the CUDA graph and is not used.
  • Exact search makes objective fusion exact: adding a per-user collaborative term (β·log q from a SASRec trained in one minute) inside certified search keeps two forwards and 100% certification and lifts Office HR@10 from 0.1714 to 0.1825 (+1.11 [+0.62, +1.60]; MiniOneRec's FP32 beam decode: 0.1589) and HR@500 by 4.0 points — beam-then-rerank cannot change the candidate set. Industrial: +0.51 [+0.07, +0.95]. With random item values, LLM-top-50-then-rerank recovers only 50% of the exact top-10.
  • Same stack on Industrial (H100): MiniOneRec beam-50 semantics 8.37 ms (HR@10 0.1582) → certified with both bridges at layer 18 3.11 ms (0.1619) → + SASRec term 3.45 ms (0.1670); FP8 + fusion 3,738 users/s. On the A6000 (Office): 22.4 ms beam → 6.44 ms with fusion (HR@10 0.1825); HF generate: 869 ms.
  • Two-sided bounds: under trie normalization the best child of an n-child prefix scores at least −log n, so the set of prefixes that guarantees the certificate is known right after the first forward. It averages ≤ 1.1·K prefixes at every K up to 500 and is within 1.2–2.1× of the provable minimum for any bound-based decoder.
  • Late entry goes to the top 2 layers on Office: the third code enters at layer 26 of 28 (cascade bridge on the layer-18 first-level states), lossless against the full-depth model (ΔHR@10 +0.12 [−0.27, +0.51]); certified top-10 in 2.75 ms on an H100 (2.72 ms with the SASRec term; MiniOneRec beam-50 in the same engine and allocation: 8.43 ms; certified top-500 with fusion: 2.92 ms). Industrial loses 0.46 pt HR@1 at layer 26 and keeps layer 18.
  • Filters are exact too: with 5% of items eligible, filtering the LLM's top 50 recovers only 30.5% of the exact filtered top-10 (HR@10 0.018 vs 0.029); certified search answers in the same two forwards, 100% certified under every filter tried.
  • Model branch (mostly negative): one more epoch of supervised fine-tuning on the train split adds +0.8 HR@10 and +2.0 HR@50 to the released checkpoint (with the SASRec term: HR@10 0.1852, full depth), but a loss aligned with the decoder's restricted normalization is no better than a full-vocabulary control (ΔHR@10 +0.14 [−0.16, +0.47]).
  • Throughput: dynamic per-token FP8 is quality-neutral (static FP8 lost 2.4 points) and with layer-26 entry reaches 3,171 users/s on one H100 (FP16 at layer 22: 2,528).
  • Novelty check (five literature sweeps, ~400 papers, research/lit2/): the certificate is classical and was stated for GR as post-hoc beam completion (Beyond the Beam, Sep 2026); trie renormalization appears in ZeroGR; late injection of the previous SID exists in trained architectures (GR4AD LazyAR). Ours: certificate as the decoder, two-sided bounds, frozen-model late entry, exact fused objectives.
  • New negatives: choosing the prefix rows inside forward 1 from a tuned-lens estimate (one-forward decoding) covers the certificate set for only 33–78% of users; custom Triton weight-streaming GEMMs lose to cuBLAS at ≥ 64 rows; GemLite INT8 is slower; max-autotune and PDL change latency by ≤ 2.4%.
  • Certified exact decoding replaces beam search: one forward scores the prompt with every first code, a second scores the best-bounded prefixes, and an on-device test proves the top-K is the global optimum (0 violations against brute force, FP32).
  • The certificate's cost scales with K, not the catalog: under trie scoring certifying the top K expands about K prefixes at every K up to 1,000, and only multi-child prefixes need the second forward. Certified top-500: 3.8 ms on an H100 vs 18.1 ms for MiniOneRec's beam-500; 6.4× its throughput.
  • Late entry: the third SID code does not need the lower 22 layers. A bridge distilled from the parent node's layer-22 state (validation prompts, no labels, 1–2 minutes) matches the full-depth second forward on HR@10 and NDCG@10 on both datasets; early exit at the same cost loses 2.4–3.7 points of HR@1.
  • Industrial & Scientific: certified decoding with late entry matches MiniOneRec's own beam-50 (HR@10 0.1597 vs 0.1582, n.s.) at 9.4 vs 25.8 ms on the A6000 and 3.2 vs 8.4 ms on the H100.
  • The scoring rule is a first-order choice: on Office a validation-chosen codebook objective lifts HR@10 from 0.159 to 0.170 and HR@500 by 2.4 points; on Industrial nothing beats the original objective, and certified decoding matches it.
  • The beam-search curse grows with list length: under MiniOneRec's own objective exact search loses 1.8 / 2.5 / 3.5 / 4.3 points of HR@50 / 100 / 200 / 500 on Office. Beam search's prefix-first pruning acts as an implicit depth discount.
  • Negative results: one forward is not enough (a user-independent third-code table loses 4.8 points of HR@1; speculating the expansion set covers at most 29% of requests); late entry of the second code is a trade-off; INT8/INT4 weight-only and FP8 do not help at these shapes.

How MiniOneRec decodes, and why it was slow

MiniOneRec turns a user's history into a prompt of about 100 tokens and asks a 1.5B-parameter Qwen2 model to write the next item's semantic ID: three code tokens <a><b><c>, then a forced newline and end-of-sequence. A catalog trie allows only codes that exist (88 first codes, 2,488 two-code prefixes, 3,444 items). Beam search keeps the 50 best partial IDs at each step. An item's score is the sum of five log-probabilities, each normalized over the whole 152k-token vocabulary.

At batch size 1 every forward pass has to stream all ~3 GB of BF16 weights from GPU memory. On an RTX A6000 that alone takes about 4 ms, and a forward with 50 beam rows costs about the same as one with 1 row. Beam search makes five sequential forwards, so it is bound by how many times it re-reads the weights, not by the beam width. Hugging Face generate adds Python overhead on top and lands at 869 ms.

What changed

  • One CUDA graph per request. A hand-written Qwen2 forward and a GPU-resident constrained beam search run the whole request — prompt, three code steps, the forced suffix and the final sort — as one replay.
  • Fused forced suffix. The newline and EOS are forced, so both are scored in one two-token forward instead of two (−10 ms).
  • Trie-level prefill fusion. All 88 first codes are appended to the prompt as tree nodes (each sees the prompt and itself). One forward gives the probability of every first code and of every second code under it. This removes another sequential step and is exact.
  • Triton shared-prefix attention. One kernel per layer attends the 50 beam rows to a single copy of the prompt's keys and values plus each row's few private tokens.
  • Certified exact decoding. Every term is a log-probability, so it is ≤ 0. The score of a first-two-code prefix therefore upper-bounds the score of every item under it. After the fused forward, every item has a bound. One more forward scores the best-bounded prefixes exactly. If the K-th exact score beats every remaining bound, the returned top-K is provably the best possible under the model's own scoring rule; no beam search is needed.

What exact decoding revealed

To test what exact search changes, we computed the FP32 log-probability of every catalog item for every test user on both datasets (about 16 million items per dataset) and replayed every search and scoring choice offline. Under MiniOneRec's own score, beam-50 misses 14% of the true top-10 on Office (6% on Industrial), yet finding the true optimum does not raise HR@10. Search error is not what limits quality.

The scoring rule is what limits quality. The third code's log-probability falls by about 1.6 nats when an item shares its two-code prefix with even one sibling, and roughly half of the next-token mass leaks onto tokens that cannot be valid codes. On Office, a per-codebook softmax with the third code weighted 0.5 — chosen on the separate validation split — improves HR@10 by 1.1 points and NDCG@10 by 0.6 points. On Industrial the same tuning finds nothing better than the original rule, and dropping the forced newline/EOS terms there hurts. The rule is a real, dataset-dependent choice that should be made on validation data, and certified decoding makes any such rule exact.

Caveats

  • One model family (MiniOneRec, Qwen2-1.5B) and small catalogs; exact decoding cost grows with the number of first-level codes and with how loose the prefix bound is. The certificate tells you per request when it did not close.
  • Trie, codebook and weighted scoring change the decoding objective (a decode-time choice, like FlashRec's per-codebook softmax); full-semantics numbers are reported separately (26.1 ms in FP16, top-1 identical to FP32 for 500/500 users).
  • Industrial latencies on this page are from the A6000; an earlier Industrial run on an L40S measured 11.5 ms for certified decoding.
  • Quality is SID-level HR/NDCG; 15 catalog SIDs are shared by two items. Latency is batch 1 on one RTX A6000 with setup, compilation and graph capture excluded.
